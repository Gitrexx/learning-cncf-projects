cert-manager

The last three deep-dives (Falco, OPA, Kyverno) were all about the same question: should this object be allowed to exist? cert-manager is a sideways move into a different corner of cluster security — not "is this request allowed," but "does every service that needs a TLS certificate actually have a valid, unexpired one, right now, without a human copying files around." It turns X.509 certificate issuance and renewal — historically a slow, manual, easy-to- forget-until-an-outage process — into a Kubernetes-native, declarative, self-renewing control loop.

Where this fits cert-manager sits underneath almost everything else in this landscape that needs TLS: Ingress controllers terminating HTTPS, Istio/Linkerd sidecars doing mTLS, internal APIs that want encrypted service-to-service traffic. Where Kyverno and OPA decide whether an object is admitted, cert-manager keeps a completely different kind of promise running in the background — that a Secret full of certificate material stays valid — with no admission webhook involved at all. It's the plumbing every other security control quietly depends on.

The problem: certificates rot, and humans forget

Before cert-manager, getting HTTPS onto a Kubernetes-hosted service meant someone manually requesting a certificate from a CA (internal or public), pasting the key material into a Secret, and — critically — remembering to repeat that whole process before the certificate expired. Public CAs and Let's Encrypt in particular push toward short-lived certificates (Let's Encrypt's default is 90 days) specifically to shrink the blast radius of a compromised key, which only makes the manual-renewal problem worse: the shorter the validity window, the more often a human has to remember to act, and the more often "outage caused by an expired cert nobody rotated" shows up in a postmortem.

cert-manager's answer is to make a certificate a Kubernetes resource with a reconciling controller behind it, exactly like a Deployment keeps N replicas running. You declare the certificate you want; cert-manager continuously drives reality toward that declaration — requesting it, storing it in a Secret, and re-requesting it automatically as the expiry approaches — for as long as the resource exists.

Five CRDs, one reconciliation loop

cert-manager runs as a controller (plus a webhook component for CRD validation/defaulting, and a CA-injector for other components' webhook bundles) watching a small family of custom resources. Only two of them are ones you typically write by hand — the rest are generated and driven by the controller itself:

ResourceScopeRole
Issuer / ClusterIssuernamespaced / cluster-wideYou write this. Represents a certificate authority cert-manager can talk to — ACME, an internal CA keypair, HashiCorp Vault, Venafi, self-signed, or an out-of-tree issuer. Issuer can only be referenced by Certificates in its own namespace; ClusterIssuer can be referenced from anywhere.
CertificatenamespacedYou write this. Declares the certificate you want — DNS names, duration, renewal window, target Secret name, and a reference to an Issuer/ClusterIssuer. The thing you actually manage day to day.
CertificateRequestnamespacedGenerated by cert-manager for you. A single, immutable signing request — a CSR plus an issuer reference — that a given Issuer implementation fulfills. Also usable directly by other tooling (e.g. kubectl cert-manager CLI, cert-manager's CSI drivers) that wants a cert without a full Certificate object.

The chain is strictly one-directional: Certificate → CertificateRequest → (for ACME) Order → one or more Challenges. You never create a CertificateRequest, Order, or Challenge yourself when using the normal Certificate workflow — cert-manager's controllers own their entire lifecycle, including deleting them once they've served their purpose.

Issuer types: same CRD shape, very different backends

An Issuer/ClusterIssuer's spec holds exactly one issuer backend. Which one you pick determines how a CertificateRequest actually gets signed:

Issuer typeWhat it does
acmeTalks to any RFC 8555 ACME server (Let's Encrypt, ZeroSSL, Google Trust Services, a private ACME CA like step-ca or Smallstep). Drives the Order/Challenge dance below. Free, publicly trusted, short-lived certs — the default choice for anything internet-facing.
caSigns directly using a keypair you already hold in a Secret (e.g. your org's internal root or intermediate CA). No network round trip, no ACME challenges — fastest and simplest option for internal-only trust.
selfSignedEach certificate signs itself — no CA at all. Mostly for bootstrapping (e.g. minting the CA keypair that a ca-type Issuer will then use) or throwaway dev/test TLS.
vaultDelegates signing to a HashiCorp Vault PKI secrets engine — Vault stays the CA of record and source of audit trail; cert-manager just automates the request/renew cycle against it.
venafiIntegrates with Venafi's enterprise certificate lifecycle platform — for organizations whose CA governance already runs through Venafi.
external / out-of-treeAny controller implementing cert-manager's CertificateRequest approval/signing contract — cloud-provider CAs like AWS Private CA or Google CAS ship as separate external issuer controllers rather than built-ins.

The ACME path in detail: Order, Challenge, self-check

ACME is the interesting case because, unlike a ca issuer, cert-manager can't just sign — it has to prove domain ownership to a third party first. When a CertificateRequest references an ACME issuer, cert-manager:

  1. Creates an Order, which registers the request with the ACME server and gets back one "authorization" per DNS name that needs proving.
  2. Creates one Challenge per authorization, each picking a solver — HTTP-01 or DNS-01 — based on your Issuer's solvers config (which can vary per DNS name via selectors).
  3. Presents the challenge: for HTTP-01, cert-manager spins up a temporary Pod + Service + Ingress path that serves a computed token at http://<domain>/.well-known/acme-challenge/<token>; for DNS-01, it calls your configured DNS provider's API (Route53, Cloud DNS, Cloudflare, and 15+ others out of the box, plus a webhook interface for any provider without native support) to create a _acme-challenge.<domain> TXT record with the computed value.
  4. Self-checks — cert-manager itself polls the HTTP path or does the DNS lookup, retrying on a fixed interval, before ever telling the ACME server to look. This avoids wasting one of the ACME server's (rate-limited) validation attempts on a record that hasn't propagated yet. (For networks where cert-manager can't observe the same path the ACME server will — NAT, split-horizon DNS — waitInsteadOfSelfCheck swaps this for a fixed wait instead.)
  5. Once the self-check passes, cert-manager tells the ACME server the challenge is ready; the ACME server independently re-checks the same HTTP path or DNS record from the outside — this out-of-band verification is the actual proof of domain ownership.
  6. With every authorization valid, cert-manager finalizes the Order — submits the CSR — and the ACME server returns a signed certificate chain.
  7. The HTTP-01 solver Pod/Ingress or DNS-01 TXT record is cleaned up, and the signed chain flows back up into the CertificateRequest, then into the target Secret.

Notice what changes between HTTP-01 and DNS-01 Both solve the exact same problem — "prove you control this DNS name" — via completely different mechanics: HTTP-01 needs a publicly reachable path on port 80 for that domain (so it can't validate a wildcard like *.example.com, and it fails behind most NAT/firewall setups), while DNS-01 only needs API credentials for your DNS provider (so it works for wildcards and fully private services with no public endpoint at all, at the cost of granting cert-manager write access to your DNS zone). This is the same trade-off you'll see again with SPIFFE/SPIRE's node vs. workload attestation later in this section: different evidence, same underlying question of "prove who you are."

Renewal: a percentage, not a date

A Certificate's spec.renewBefore (an absolute duration like 360h) or spec.renewBeforePercentage (e.g. 33, meaning "renew once two-thirds of the way through the validity window") tells cert-manager how early to start the whole issuance flow again — before the current certificate actually expires. renewBeforePercentage is the recommended default because a fixed absolute duration can misfire if the CA hands back a shorter validity period than you requested (a real risk with some ACME CAs), while a percentage always scales correctly to whatever duration was actually issued.

Renewal reuses the identical Certificate → CertificateRequest → (Order/Challenge) pipeline shown above — there's no separate "renewal" code path. The one variable is spec.privateKey.rotationPolicy: Never reuses the existing private key across reissuance (new cert, same key), while Always generates a fresh key every time. As of cert-manager v1.18, Always is the default, precisely because a key that never rotates is a key whose exposure — even years after a leak nobody noticed — silently compounds; a fresh key on every reissuance bounds how long any single leaked key stays useful. Either way, the target Secret's tls.crt/tls.key are overwritten in place — cert-manager also enforces any secretTemplate labels/annotations you configured, re-reconciling them if something else edits the Secret.

Beyond the plain Secret: trust distribution and short-lived workload certs

A few sibling projects extend cert-manager past "one Secret per Certificate":

  • trust-manager — reconciles a Bundle CRD that assembles trusted CA certificates from multiple sources and distributes the combined trust bundle as a ConfigMap/Secret into every namespace that needs it — the "everyone needs to trust our internal CA" problem cert-manager itself doesn't solve.
  • csi-driver / csi-driver-spiffe — mount a live, auto-renewing certificate straight into a Pod as an ephemeral volume instead of a Secret, so the key material never touches the Kubernetes API or etcd at all; csi-driver-spiffe specifically issues SVIDs in SPIFFE's format, previewing the identity model the SPIFFE/SPIRE deep-dives cover next.
  • istio-csr — plugs cert-manager in as the CA backing Istio's mTLS, so the same Issuer infrastructure (Vault, an internal CA, ACME) that issues your Ingress certificates can also issue the short-lived workload certs Istio's sidecars use for mesh mTLS.
Often confused with HashiCorp Vault can itself act as a full PKI CA — but cert-manager isn't competing with it; a vault-type Issuer lets cert-manager drive Vault's PKI engine the same declarative way it drives Let's Encrypt. SPIFFE/SPIRE (next in this section) solve workload identity specifically — short-lived SVIDs issued via attestation, no ACME-style domain-ownership proof involved — while cert-manager is CA-agnostic and general-purpose, happy to issue a public-facing Ingress cert, an internal mTLS cert, or (via csi-driver-spiffe) a SPIFFE-shaped one. And cert-manager only ever issues and renews certificates — it has no opinion on how they're used at the network layer; that's Envoy/Istio/Linkerd's job (all covered earlier in this section).

Why it graduated, and where you'll meet it

cert-manager was accepted into CNCF (as jetstack/cert-manager, its originating company) in November 2020, reached Incubating in September 2022, and reached Graduated status on September 29, 2024 — announced that November at KubeCon NA. The graduation case leaned heavily on sheer ubiquity: over 500 million downloads a month, and CNCF's own end-user research finding it deployed on roughly 86% of newly created production clusters. You'll meet cert-manager as the thing quietly renewing the Let's Encrypt certificate behind almost every ingress-nginx or cloud load balancer TLS setup, as the CA backing internal mTLS for a service mesh, and — increasingly — as infrastructure other CNCF projects build on rather than reimplement, exactly the role trust-manager and istio-csr play above.
