Knative

Dapr, the last deep-dive, gave application code a uniform API for distributed-systems plumbing — but it had nothing to say about how many copies of your app are running. That's the layer Knative fills: a request-driven runtime on top of Kubernetes that decides pod count for you, all the way down to zero, and gives every deployment a URL, a traffic-split router, and a history of revisions for free. It's what people mean when they say "serverless on Kubernetes" — not because it hides Kubernetes, but because it automates the one thing Kubernetes' own primitives were never designed to do well: scaling a stateless HTTP workload to exactly the load it's getting, including none at all.

Where this fits Knative has two independent halves under one project umbrella. Knative Serving runs request-driven containers with automatic scale-to-zero and traffic-splitting — this deep-dive's main focus. Knative Eventing is a separate CloudEvents-native plumbing layer for connecting event producers to consumers without either side knowing about the other. You can run Serving without Eventing (most people do, for plain HTTP services) or Eventing without Serving (event routing to any addressable target) — they compose, but neither depends on the other.

The problem: a Deployment doesn't know it's serving HTTP requests

A Kubernetes Deployment plus a Service gets you a running, load-balanced workload — but every scaling decision has to be bolted on separately, and none of the defaults fit a bursty, request-driven web service well. The Horizontal Pod Autoscaler scales on CPU/memory by default — a weak proxy for "how many requests are actually arriving" — and it can never go below one replica, so every service pays for at least one always-on pod even at 3am with zero traffic. Rolling out a new version means juggling two Deployments and a manual traffic split if you want a canary. None of this is wrong, exactly — it's just infrastructure every team building request-driven services ends up re-inventing slightly differently, the same shape of problem Dapr solved for state/pub-sub/service-calls one layer over. Knative, started at Google in 2018 with early contributions from IBM, Red Hat, VMware, and SAP, standardizes it: give it a container image, and it handles the URL, the routing, the revisions, and the scale curve — including all the way to zero.

The object model: Service → Configuration → Revision, with a Route on top

Applying a Knative Service (often called a ksvc to avoid confusing it with the Kubernetes Service it sits above) creates three more objects underneath, and understanding what each one owns explains almost everything else about how Knative behaves:

ObjectWhat it representsMutability
Service The resource you actually apply — owns a Configuration and a Route, and gets one stable URL for the life of the app. Mutable; editing its spec.template is how you deploy a new version.
Configuration The desired state you're currently running: image, env vars, resource requests, concurrency settings. Mutable; every change stamps out a brand-new Revision.
Revision An immutable point-in-time snapshot of a Configuration — a specific code+config version, scaled independently of every other Revision of the same Service. Immutable — created once, eventually garbage-collected; never edited.
Route Maps percentages of traffic, under the Service's one URL, across one or more Revisions. Mutable — this is the mechanism behind canaries, blue/green cutovers, and instant rollbacks.

Because old Revisions stick around (until garbage-collected) and each keeps its own independent scale state, a rollback is just moving the Route's 100% back to the previous Revision — no rebuild, no redeploy, and the old Revision's pods spin back up from wherever its own scale-to-zero state left them.

The request path: queue-proxy, the Activator, and getting out of the way

Every Revision pod runs your container plus a sidecar called queue-proxy, which sits in front of your container, enforces the Revision's configured concurrency limit, and reports per-pod request metrics upward. In front of the whole Revision sits a pluggable networking layer (Kourier by default, or Istio, Contour, Envoy Gateway, and others — the same swappable-implementation pattern as Dapr's components) that turns Route objects into real ingress config. The piece that makes scale-to-zero actually work without dropping requests is the Activator:

When a Revision has zero ready pods, the networking layer routes traffic to the Activator instead of a pod. The Activator holds the request in a buffer, reports the pending demand to the Autoscaler, and waits for a pod to become ready — a cold start — before forwarding it on. Once at least one pod is ready and has spare capacity, the Activator steps back out of the path and the networking layer sends traffic straight to pods again — it's a purely on-demand component, not a permanent proxy hop.

The Knative Pod Autoscaler: target concurrency, stable and panic windows

The default autoscaler — KPA, Knative Pod Autoscaler (you can swap in the plain Kubernetes HPA, or KEDA, as alternate autoscaler classes for CPU- or event-based scaling instead) — scales on concurrency: how many in-flight requests each pod is handling right now, against a configured target (100 by default, tunable per Revision). It evaluates two overlapping time windows on every tick:

  • Stable window (60s default) — the primary signal: average observed concurrency over the last minute, used for ordinary, damped scaling decisions that don't overreact to a single noisy sample.
  • Panic window (6s default, 10% of the stable window) — a short-term signal that exists purely to catch sudden spikes fast. If observed concurrency in the panic window exceeds 2× the target, KPA enters panic mode: it scales up on the fast 6-second signal instead of waiting out the full minute, and — critically — it refuses to scale back down again until the burst has fully subsided for a whole stable window, so a spiky-but-brief traffic pattern doesn't thrash pods up and down every few seconds.

Scaling down is deliberately gradual (damped within the stable window) all the way to one pod; going from one pod to zero is a separate, final step gated by its own scale-to-zero-grace-period (30s default) — a last bit of idle time held in reserve in case traffic resumes, before the last pod is actually torn down and the Activator becomes the sole path for the next request.

What to notice The jump from 0 pods only ever happens through the Activator buffering at least one tick's worth of requests during the cold start — that buffering is what stops a scale-from-zero request from simply failing. Scaling down is the gradual, damped path: pods ease down one at a time, and the last pod gets a full grace-period hold before it's actually removed. Panic mode is the odd one out — it's the only case where pods jump immediately, and it comes with a temporary promise not to scale down again right away, trading a few extra warm pods for protection against thrashing.

Knative Eventing: routing CloudEvents without producer/consumer coupling

Eventing solves a different problem: getting an event from whatever produced it to whatever should react to it, in a common envelope, without either side hardcoding the other's existence. Every event on the wire is a CloudEvent, and four object kinds do the routing:

ObjectRole
SourceA CRD wrapping an event producer — PingSource (cron), ApiServerSource (Kubernetes API events), KafkaSource, and dozens more — that emits CloudEvents to a configured sink.
BrokerAn event mesh entry point: producers send to it, consumers subscribe to it via Triggers; handles retry and dead-lettering with at-least-once delivery.
TriggerA filter on a Broker's event stream (by CloudEvent type, source, or custom extension attributes) that forwards only matching events to a target.
Channel & SubscriptionA lower-level, pluggable fan-out primitive (backed by Kafka, NATS, or an in-memory implementation) that a Broker is often itself built on top of.

Because every hop speaks the same CloudEvents envelope over HTTP, a Source has no idea what's consuming its events, and a Trigger's target — often a Knative Service — has no idea what produced them. That target can itself be any addressable resource, including a plain Knative Service, so an event can be the thing that scales a Serving Revision up from zero in the first place.

Often confused with Knative vs. KEDA: KPA (Knative's default autoscaler) is purpose-built for request-driven HTTP concurrency and ships its own Activator/buffering path for true zero-drop scale-from-zero; KEDA is a general event-driven autoscaler for arbitrary Deployments driven by queue depth, Kafka lag, or 60+ other sources — and Knative can actually use KEDA as an alternate autoscaler class when the workload should scale on something other than request concurrency. Knative vs. a plain Deployment + HPA: you get scale-to-zero, immutable Revisions, and built-in traffic-splitting for free — none of which the HPA alone provides. Knative vs. Dapr (previous deep-dive): Dapr is about what a running workload can call (state, pub/sub, service invocation); Knative is about how the workload runs (request-driven autoscaling) — they compose, since a Knative Service can carry a Dapr sidecar. Knative Eventing vs. a message broker like Kafka: Eventing doesn't replace a broker — Channels are often backed by one; it adds the CloudEvents-native routing/filtering layer (Broker + Trigger) on top.

Why it graduated, and where you'll meet it

Knative started at Google in 2018, hit 1.0 in 2021 as a first production-readiness milestone, joined CNCF as an Incubating project in 2022, and reached Graduated status on October 8, 2025 — one of the newest graduations in the landscape. CNCF pointed to its normalized governance (the earlier separate working groups merged into a single Steering Committee by December 2024), its defined maintainer lifecycle and annual elections, and adoption spanning managed platforms (Red Hat OpenShift Serverless, IBM Cloud Code Engine, VMware Tanzu, SAP BTP) as evidence it had matured into shared, vendor-neutral infrastructure rather than one company's serverless bet. In production you'll meet it as the engine under managed "serverless container" offerings, and directly wherever a platform team wants request-driven autoscaling — including real scale-to-zero for bursty or low-traffic internal services — without hand-building an Activator-shaped buffering layer themselves.
