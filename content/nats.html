NATS

Sooner or later every microservice platform needs services to talk to each other without holding a hard-coded address. That means fire-and-forget events, "ask anyone who can answer," work queues, and durable streams that survive a consumer being down. Most teams end up with Kafka for one part, RabbitMQ for another, and a sidecar or service mesh for the rest. NATS tries to cover all of it with one small Go binary (about 20 MB, no ZooKeeper, no JVM). The core is a very fast subject-based router that gives at-most-once pub/sub, request-reply, and load-balanced queue groups. JetStream is a persistence layer built into the same server. It adds streams, replayable consumers, acknowledgements, and a Key-Value and Object Store on top.

Where this fits NATS was written by Derek Collison around 2010 as the message bus inside Cloud Foundry. It started in Ruby and was rewritten in Go in 2012. It joined the CNCF as an incubating project in 2018 and graduated at KubeCon + CloudNativeCon North America 2026, after a governance dispute in 2025 that nearly took it out of the foundation (covered below). You'll find it as the control-plane bus in edge and IoT fleets, as the event backbone for trading, gaming and telco systems, inside platforms like Dapr (as a pub/sub component), and more and more as the messaging layer between AI agents. Relative to Kubernetes, NATS is just a workload. You run it as a StatefulSet, usually from the official Helm chart. It doesn't extend the Kubernetes API. Instead it gives your Pods a shared address space of subjects.

The problem: addresses are the wrong abstraction

In Kubernetes you reach a service through its Service DNS name. That's point-to-point. The caller has to know who it's talking to, and it has to wait. Three needs keep coming up that plain HTTP handles badly:

  • Fan-out. "An order was created." Billing, shipping, analytics, and the audit log all care, and the publisher shouldn't have to know about any of them.
  • Location independence. "Somebody price this cart." Any healthy instance in any cluster or region should be able to answer, and the caller shouldn't need a load balancer or service discovery to find one.
  • Temporal decoupling. "Process these when you can." The consumer may be down, deploying, or slow. The messages have to wait for it and be replayed in order.

Before NATS (and alongside it) teams reached for a broker for each need. NATS makes the subject the address. Publishers send to a name like orders.eu.created, subscribers express interest in patterns, and the server's only job is matching the two. Where the subscriber runs (which Pod, cluster, or continent) is the server's concern, not the application's.

Core NATS: subjects, interest, and nothing else

A subject is a dot-separated string of tokens. Nobody creates it ahead of time. Subscriptions can use two wildcards:

WildcardMatchesExample
*Exactly one token, in any positionorders.*.created matches orders.eu.created, but not orders.eu.de.created
>One or more tokens, and only as the last tokenorders.> matches orders.eu and orders.eu.de.created, but not bare orders

Clients speak a tiny line-based text protocol over TCP (port 4222). After a CONNECT handshake, the whole protocol is basically PUB, SUB, UNSUB, MSG, and PING/PONG. You can drive it with telnet: 

SUB orders.*.created 1          # subscribe, sid=1
PUB orders.eu.created 11        # publish an 11-byte payload
{"id":4711}
MSG orders.eu.created 1 11      # server delivers to sid 1
{"id":4711}

The delivery guarantee is at-most-once. The server keeps an in-memory interest graph (a subject trie with a cache in front of it). When a message arrives it is written to the sockets of whoever is interested right now, and then it's gone. If nobody is subscribed, the message is dropped. There's no disk and no ack. This is a deliberate choice: it's why core NATS can move millions of messages per second per server with sub-millisecond latency, and why the server can protect itself against a slow consumer by simply disconnecting it.

Request-reply without a load balancer

Request-reply is built from plain pub/sub. The requester creates a unique inbox subject, subscribes to it, and publishes the request with that inbox set as the reply-to field. Any responder publishes its answer to the reply subject. The requester takes the first reply and moves on. If no one is subscribed to the request subject, the server tells the client right away with a no-responders status (a 503 header), so the caller doesn't sit through a timeout.

Queue groups: load balancing for free

Subscribers that join with the same queue group name share the subscription. Each message goes to one random member of the group. Plain subscribers and other groups still get their own copy. So for any one message the rule is: every plain matching subscriber gets it, plus exactly one member of each matching queue group. Scaling a worker Deployment from 3 to 30 replicas needs no configuration. The new Pods just join the group. Combine that with request-reply and you have service discovery and load balancing in one primitive.

JetStream: persistence inside the same server

  

    Core NATS is deliberately forgetful. JetStream (enabled with -js or a
    jetstream {} config block) adds durability without bolting on a second system. It replaced the
    older, separate NATS Streaming / STAN project, which is now end-of-life. JetStream has two central objects.
  

  

    A stream captures messages published on a set of subjects and stores them in order, each with
    a sequence number. Publishers don't change anything. They still publish to orders.eu.created.
    Because a stream is listening on that subject, the server persists the message and replies with a
    PubAck.
    Streams have limits (max messages, bytes, age, messages per subject) and a retention policy:
  

  

    
RetentionMessages are removed when…Use it for

    
      
limits (default)a limit (age, count, bytes) is reached, like a Kafka topicevent log, replay, audit

      
interestevery consumer that exists has acked themfan-out that shouldn't pile up

      
workqueueany one consumer has acked them (each message is processed once)job queues, like a classic RabbitMQ queue

    
  

  

    A consumer is a server-side view into a stream: a cursor, a filter, and delivery state.
    The server tracks it, not the client. That's the key difference from Kafka, where consumers commit their own
    offsets. The main knobs:
  

  
    
    
  • Durable vs. ephemeral. A durable consumer has a name and keeps its position across
      disconnects (our billing). An ephemeral one disappears when inactive.
    • 
    
  • Pull vs. push. Pull consumers Fetch batches when they're ready. This is the
      recommended default and scales out naturally: several Pods pulling from the same durable consumer share the
      work. Push consumers have the server send to a delivery subject.
    • 
    
  • DeliverPolicy. Where to start: all, last, new,
      by_start_sequence, by_start_time, or last_per_subject.
    • 
    
  • AckPolicy, AckWait, MaxDeliver. With explicit acks, a message that isn't acked
      within AckWait (default 30s) is redelivered, up to MaxDeliver times. Clients can
      also Nak (redeliver now or after a delay), send InProgress to extend the deadline,
      or Term to give up. MaxAckPending limits how many unacked messages can be in flight,
      which gives you backpressure.
    • 
  


  

    "Exactly once," precisely
    JetStream gives you at-least-once delivery, plus two features that together get you effectively-once.
    On the publish side, set a Nats-Msg-Id header. The stream drops any repeat of that ID within its
    duplicate window,
    so a publisher that retries after a lost PubAck doesn't store the message twice. On the consume side,
    double ack (AckSync) waits for the server to confirm it recorded the ack, so you know the
    message won't come back. Recent releases have added more stream-level primitives. 2.12 brought
    atomic batch publish (a set of messages stored all-or-nothing), distributed
    counters (Nats-Incr headers), and delayed message scheduling. 2.14 added faster batch
    commits.
  


  
Replication: Raft, many times over

  

    Each stream has a replica count, R1, R3, or R5. A replicated stream is
    its own Raft
    group: one server is the stream leader, writes are committed once a quorum has them, and if the leader dies a
    follower takes over. Every replicated consumer is a separate Raft group too. On top of these sits the
    meta group, a cluster-wide Raft group with a "meta leader" that handles the JetStream API
    (create stream, create consumer) and decides placement. So a cluster with 200 R3 streams runs hundreds of
    small Raft groups instead of one big log. If you've read the etcd deep-dive, this is the same quorum logic,
    sharded per stream.
  


  
Key-Value and Object Store: streams in disguise

  

    The Key-Value store is a stream named KV_<bucket> that captures
    $KV.<bucket>.>. A put publishes to $KV.config.db.url.
    MaxMsgsPerSubject sets how many historical revisions you keep, and a get reads the
    last message for that subject. Because it's a stream you get watch (a consumer on the key pattern),
    compare-and-set (via the expected-last-sequence header), TTLs, and history at no extra cost. It works like a
    lightweight etcd for application config, feature flags, and leader election. The Object Store
    does the same thing for large blobs. It chunks a file into many messages in an OBJ_<bucket>
    stream and keeps a metadata entry with a digest.
  


  
Topology: clusters, superclusters, leaf nodes

  
  
    
    
  • Cluster. Servers connect to each other in a full mesh of routes and share
      subscription interest, so a client can connect to any node. Clients get the list of cluster members
      automatically and reconnect elsewhere on failure. In Kubernetes this is the Helm chart's StatefulSet plus a
      headless Service.
    • 
    
  • Supercluster. Clusters joined by gateways. Gateways forward a message only to
      clusters that have interest in it, and they learn that interest gradually rather than flooding it. That makes
      multi-region practical. A request from us-east can be answered by a queue member in
      eu-west when no local member exists.
    • 
    
  • Leaf node. A server, possibly on a Raspberry Pi in a shop or a truck, that dials
      out to a hub. It is a single outbound connection (good for NAT and firewalls), it can run its own
      local JetStream, and it keeps serving local clients while the uplink is down. Streams can then
      mirror or source data between edge and core. This is the main reason NATS shows up in edge
      architectures, alongside KubeEdge.
    • 
  


  
Security: accounts and decentralized JWT auth

  

    Multi-tenancy is built in. An account is an isolated subject namespace: orders.>
    in account A and in account B are entirely separate. Accounts share data only through explicit
    exports and imports of streams (message flows) or services (request-reply
    endpoints), which can be remapped to other subject names along the way.
  

  

    Identity uses a three-level chain of NKeys
    and JWTs: an operator signs account JWTs, and an account signs
    user JWTs that carry permissions (which subjects the user may publish or subscribe to). The
    server only needs to trust the operator's public key. It then verifies any user by checking signatures, and
    a client proves possession of its key by signing a server-issued nonce. "Decentralized" means a tenant can
    create its own users offline, with the nsc tool, without touching the server config. Account JWTs
    are distributed through a resolver, either the built-in one or an account server. For integrating with an
    existing IdP there's auth callout, where the server delegates the auth decision to a service
    you write. TLS and mTLS are supported on every port.
  


  
Running it on Kubernetes


helm repo add nats https://nats-io.github.io/k8s/helm/charts/
helm install nats nats/nats \
  --set config.cluster.enabled=true --set config.cluster.replicas=3 \
  --set config.jetstream.enabled=true \
  --set config.jetstream.fileStore.pvc.size=10Gi

# then, from a debug pod or the nats-box deployment:
nats stream add ORDERS --subjects 'orders.>' --replicas 3 --storage file
nats consumer add ORDERS billing --pull --filter 'orders.*.created' --ack explicit
nats pub orders.eu.created '{"id":4711}'
nats consumer next ORDERS billing --ack

  

    The chart runs a StatefulSet with a PVC per Pod for JetStream file storage, a headless Service for routes, a
    client Service on 4222, and a nats-box utility Pod with the nats CLI. If you'd rather
    manage streams GitOps-style, NACK (NATS Controllers for Kubernetes) provides
    Stream, Consumer, KeyValue, and ObjectStore CRDs that a
    controller reconciles into the JetStream API, so Argo CD or Flux can own them. Monitoring is on port
    8222 (/varz, /connz, /jsz), and the
    prometheus-nats-exporter sidecar exposes it to Prometheus.

The 2025 governance fight

  

    The project's history is part of why its graduation stood out. In April 2025, Synadia, the
    company founded by Collison that employs most of the maintainers, told the CNCF it wanted to take NATS back,
    and signaled it might relicense the server under the source-available Business Source License. The CNCF
    publicly objected. The NATS trademark and the nats.io domain had never been formally handed over,
    and the CNCF argued that leaving would break the promises made when the project joined. On May 1,
    2025 the two sides reached an agreement. Synadia transferred the NATS trademarks to the Linux
    Foundation, and NATS stayed in the CNCF under Apache-2.0. Synadia still leads development and
    sells commercial offerings around it. For adopters, the result is a vendor-neutral trademark and an open
    license that can't be changed unilaterally, which is exactly what graduation is meant to certify.
  


  
Why it graduated

  

    Graduation means broad production adoption, a healthy multi-company contributor base, a security audit, and
    mature governance. NATS had the adoption for years, across finance, telco, automotive, retail edge, and many
    platforms that embed it. It has more than 40 official and community client libraries. The 2025 agreement
    settled the last open question, governance. When CNCF announced the graduation at KubeCon NA 2026, it framed
    NATS as a production-ready communication layer between workloads and, increasingly, AI agents. When you see
    "NATS" in a blog post, read it as: a lightweight, subject-addressed message bus that also does streaming,
    and stretches from a single edge box to a global supercluster.
