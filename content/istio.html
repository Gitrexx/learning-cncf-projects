Istio

Envoy gives every service a capable proxy sitting next to it. But a fleet of a thousand identical Envoys still needs someone to decide, consistently, what routing rules, mTLS policy, and retry budgets each one should run — and to keep pushing updates as Pods come and go. Istio is that someone: a control plane that turns a pile of independent Envoy proxies into one coherently-managed service mesh. It's the most feature-complete mesh in the CNCF landscape, and the one most people mean by default when they say "service mesh."

Where this fits Istio started as a joint project between Google, IBM, and Lyft in 2017 (Lyft contributed Envoy as the dataplane). It joined CNCF as an incubating project on September 30, 2022, and graduated on July 12, 2023 — less than a year later, the fastest graduation in CNCF history at the time, reflecting how long and widely it had already been running in production before it even joined the foundation. If Envoy is the proxy, Istio is the opinionated system that decides what every Envoy in the mesh should be doing right now.

The problem: consistent policy across hundreds of independent proxies

A single Envoy sidecar is only as useful as its configuration. Someone still has to answer, cluster-wide and continuously: which service versions exist right now, which of them should get canary traffic, which calls require mutual TLS, which callers are allowed to reach which services, and how to turn all of that into the listeners/routes/clusters/endpoints Envoy actually understands. Doing this by hand — writing static Envoy config per Pod — falls apart the moment you have more than a handful of services, because Kubernetes is constantly reshaping the fleet (rollouts, autoscaling, rescheduling) faster than a human could keep per-proxy config in sync.

Istio's job is to watch the actual state of the cluster and the mesh's declared policy, and continuously compute and stream the right Envoy configuration to the right proxies — the xDS control plane role described in the Envoy deep-dive, filled in with an opinionated, Kubernetes-native way to declare that policy as CRDs.

Control plane: istiod

Everything Istio does centrally today runs in a single binary and Deployment called istiod. Earlier Istio versions split this into separate services — Pilot (configuration/discovery), Citadel (certificate authority), Galley (config validation/ distribution), and a webhook injector — but those were consolidated into istiod years ago to cut inter-process hops and operational surface. istiod now handles three jobs at once:

  • Service discovery & config translation — watches the Kubernetes API for Services, Endpoints, and Istio's own CRDs (VirtualService, DestinationRule, and others), turns them into xDS resources, and streams them to every proxy over the same LDS/RDS/CDS/EDS/SDS protocol family Envoy speaks natively.
  • Certificate authority — issues a short-lived X.509 identity certificate to every workload (encoding its Kubernetes service account as a SPIFFE-style identity), and rotates them automatically. This is what makes mesh-wide mTLS possible without anyone hand-managing certs.
  • Configuration validation — an admission webhook that rejects malformed or conflicting Istio CRDs before they ever reach the mesh.

Data plane: sidecar mode, and now ambient mode

For most of Istio's history there was one way to get traffic into the mesh: sidecar mode. A mutating admission webhook injects an Envoy container into every meshed Pod, and iptables rules (set up by an init container, or by the newer CNI plugin approach) transparently redirect the Pod's inbound and outbound traffic through its own sidecar. The application never changes a line of code; every packet in or out just happens to pass through Envoy first.

Sidecar mode is thorough — full L7 (HTTP-aware) capability on every hop — but it means one extra container per Pod, and upgrading Envoy means restarting every workload Pod to get a new sidecar. Ambient mode, which reached general availability in Istio 1.24 (November 2024), removes the sidecar entirely:

  • ztunnel — a lightweight, purpose-built proxy written in Rust, run once per node as a DaemonSet (not per Pod). It handles L4 only: mTLS encryption, TCP-level authorization, and basic telemetry for every Pod on that node.
  • Waypoint proxies — a full Envoy instance, but scoped per namespace or service account rather than per Pod, that's only deployed when a workload actually needs L7 features (HTTP routing, retries, header-based rules). Most traffic never needs one.

istiod drives both: it pushes L4-only xDS config (identities, endpoints, mTLS policy) to ztunnel, and full Envoy xDS config (HTTP routes, VirtualService/DestinationRule rules, L7 authorization) to whichever waypoints exist. The mesh gets mTLS and L4 policy everywhere for near-zero resource cost, and pays the heavier Envoy cost only where L7 behavior is actually used — a direct answer to sidecar mode's "one Envoy per Pod, whether it needs one or not" overhead.

Zero-trust by default: identity and mTLS

Because istiod issues every workload a certificate tied to its Kubernetes service account, every mesh connection can be mutually authenticated — each side proves who it is cryptographically, not just that the packet arrived on the right port. Two CRDs control this:

  • PeerAuthentication — sets the mTLS mode (STRICT, PERMISSIVE, or DISABLE) mesh-wide, per namespace, or per workload. PERMISSIVE accepts both plaintext and mTLS, which is what makes rolling mTLS out to an existing cluster non-disruptive.
  • AuthorizationPolicy — allow/deny rules keyed on that verified identity ("only orders-service's service account may call payments's /charge endpoint"), enforced at L4 by ztunnel or at L7 by the sidecar/waypoint.

This is the "security" half of what a mesh buys you: encryption and authorization as mesh policy, not something every service team has to implement themselves.

Traffic management: VirtualService and DestinationRule

The other half — traffic management — is where Istio earns its "most feature-complete" reputation. Two CRDs work together:

CRDAnswers
VirtualServiceHow should a request to this host be routed? (path/header match, weighted split across subsets, retries, timeouts, fault injection)
DestinationRuleWhat subsets (versions) of this service exist, and what policy applies to traffic once it's routed there? (load balancing, connection pool limits, outlier detection / circuit breaking, per-subset TLS mode)

A canary rollout is the canonical example, and it's exactly what the widget below simulates: a DestinationRule declares that the reviews service has subsets v1 and v2 (selected by Pod labels), and a VirtualService splits traffic to those subsets by weight. Nothing about the application changes — you're purely reconfiguring the mesh — and shifting the weight from 100/0 toward 50/50 is a live, gradual rollout with an instant rollback path (set the weight back to 100/0).

Why it graduated, and where you'll run into it

Istio graduated on the strength of years of production use at large scale across very different organizations (Google, IBM, Salesforce, and many others) well before it formally joined CNCF, plus the breadth of what it covers — traffic management, security, and telemetry in one project — and its role as the reference implementation many newer mesh ideas (including ambient mode itself, and much of the Kubernetes Gateway API's mesh extensions) get prototyped against. In production you'll meet it as: the mesh behind progressive-delivery pipelines (canary/blue-green rollouts), the zero-trust mTLS layer inside a cluster, and increasingly as an istio GatewayClass implementing the Kubernetes Gateway API for ingress, not just mesh-internal traffic.

Often confused with Envoy is the data plane Istio drives, not a competing project — every sidecar and waypoint is Envoy, configured by istiod over xDS. Linkerd is a different service mesh entirely, built around its own minimal Rust "micro-proxy" instead of Envoy, trading some of Istio's breadth for a much smaller footprint and simpler operational model — see the next deep-dive for that comparison. The Kubernetes Gateway API is a vendor-neutral spec for describing routing; Istio can implement it (alongside its own longer-standing VirtualService/DestinationRule API), but the two aren't competitors so much as Istio slowly growing a second, more portable configuration surface next to its original one.
